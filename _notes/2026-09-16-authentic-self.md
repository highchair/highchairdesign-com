I read this while at the United Skates of America. My youngest wants to roller skate and the school has a fundraiser night. And so I blink under undulating lights while kids on wheels rocket around in a swirl while others collapse uncontrollably and lay splayed on the floor around me.

That was written by me. But did you believe it? Do you know enough about me to know that I am capable of descriptive prose like that? Or did you assume anything like that came from an A.I.?

The thesis in this article is a good one — someone is willing to offload their email responses to an A.I. but would not allow it to respond to their partner or family member. Our work lives have always been set apart from our “real” selves in the name of professionalism and work/life balance.

> The version that gets sent out to the crowd is the polished, synthetic one while the real and unedited version slowly gets pulled back and reserved for the shrinking handful of people who are still close enough to notice the difference.
{: .quote .small}

The idea that A.I. is eroding our ability to communicate authentically is false. Or at least the idea that every conversation requires your full authentic self. We never approached every interaction with complete realness, because more often than not, that is not what the conversation required.

Politicians and CEOs don’t write their own speeches or pen their own memos. But just like how some musicians don’t write their own songs, you would hope the messages are delivered with some amount of authenticity.

> As the synthetic version becomes the default state of things, the unpolished version of a person quietly starts to become valuable again via scarcity. The signals an AI cannot easily counterfeit have already started to carry a new weight. Things like a rambling voice note, a few lines in someone’s real handwriting or a reply that came too fast and too blunt to have been drafted by anything besides a human have that weight.
{: .quote .small}

And this is where it gets interesting. “Authentic” and “human-made” are becoming equated with imperfection. What about the craftspeople who value and strive for perfection? Go to any museum and look for the amazing handwork of craftspeople from centuries past. Much of it is better than machines can produce today, but doesn’t always show the work of a human hand.

As someone with certain idiosyncrasies in my typing — I often drop the apostrophe from “dont,” or mistype the word “wierd” and never go back to correct it — I like on the one hand that these may become signals that an A.I. has not polished the output. (Ostensibly, though, couldn’t you prompt A.I. to leave those artifacts alone, and even encourage a certain amount of them?) On the other hand, though, as someone who values craft and skilled artisans, I worry when imperfection becomes the goal.

All is to say that I don’t know what to do with this thought — only that I have it bouncing around in my brain as I come to terms with the way communication is evolving, along with the expectations we may continue to have about it.
